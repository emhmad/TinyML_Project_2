\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Compressing medical vision models for edge deployment is usually evaluated with
aggregate accuracy, yet in class-imbalanced clinical datasets aggregate metrics can
hide catastrophic degradation on rare, dangerous classes.  We fine-tune DeiT-Tiny
and DeiT-Small on the HAM10000 dermoscopy benchmark and evaluate three families of
compression---\textbf{pruning and sparsity}, \textbf{post-training quantization},
and \textbf{knowledge distillation}---through the lens of a newly introduced
\textbf{Dangerous-Class Degradation Ratio (DCR)} together with per-class AUROC,
expected calibration error (ECE), per-layer class-conditional gradient flow, and
quantitative attention--mask overlap.  All headline results are reported across
$n{=}4$ random seeds at the cell level and $n{=}3$ for paired significance tests,
with lesion-grouped patient splits.

Our results reshape the narrative of compression in medical vision.  \emph{Magnitude
pruning}, the default baseline in most compression papers, exhibits the most
dangerous failure mode: its DCR reaches $2.15$ on DeiT-Small, meaning dangerous
classes (\mel, \bcc, \akiec) degrade at more than twice the rate of safer classes.
\emph{Taylor (first-order) pruning}, despite its simplicity, emerges as the
Pareto-optimal clinical choice: highest melanoma AUROC ($0.928$), best calibration
(ECE $= 0.033$), and DCR below $1$ at $50\%$ sparsity.  \emph{Wanda}
(activation-aware) pruning preserves minority-class sensitivity through
\emph{classifier-head prediction collapse} ($3\times$ ECE degradation, AUROC below
magnitude's), and---contrary to our earlier qualitative reading---its
attention--mask IoU and pointing-game accuracy across $n{=}5$ seeds \emph{exceed}
magnitude's and match the dense baseline; the failure is downstream of attention,
not in feature localization.  We isolate the underlying mechanism: per-layer pruning
rate is positively correlated with per-class gradient mass for magnitude
($r \approx 0.62$--$0.74$, $p < 10^{-5}$), uncorrelated for Wanda
($|r| < 0.20$), and \emph{negatively} correlated for Taylor ($r \approx -0.41$).
Three distinct failure modes follow---magnitude's gradient-coupling, Wanda's
head-collapse, Taylor's deliberate gradient-preservation---and an earlier
activation-outlier hypothesis was disconfirmed and is preserved for transparency.
\emph{INT8 quantization} is the safest single compression lever ($3.8\times$
size reduction, no measurable clinical degradation), and \emph{knowledge
distillation} improves dense performance but does not produce pruning-robust
features.  We conclude that compression safety in medical AI cannot be inferred
from balanced accuracy or melanoma sensitivity alone, and recommend the
DCR~$+$~AUROC~$+$~ECE triplet, accompanied by per-layer gradient-coupling and
attention--mask overlap, as a minimal clinical evaluation protocol.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Dermatology is one of the clearest practical targets for edge-deployed medical AI.
Portable, offline skin-lesion classifiers could support screening in
low-connectivity settings, reduce latency, and preserve patient privacy by avoiding
cloud inference.  All of these benefits require \emph{compact} models that fit
within the memory, compute, and energy budgets of clinic-side hardware.
Compression is therefore not optional in this domain; it is the prerequisite for
deployment.

The problem is that the standard evaluation of compressed models---top-1 or
balanced accuracy on a held-out validation set---can be profoundly misleading in
clinical settings.  HAM10000, the most widely used dermoscopy benchmark, is
strongly imbalanced: melanocytic nevi dominate the training set while the deadliest
class, melanoma, is comparatively rare.  A compressed model can therefore preserve
aggregate accuracy \emph{while losing the ability to detect exactly the diseases
clinicians care most about}.  This asymmetric failure---which we term
\textbf{dangerous-class degradation}---is the central concern of this paper.

We ask: \emph{when a medical Vision Transformer is compressed, does it forget
dangerous diseases first, and does the choice of compression method determine
whether it does?}  To answer this, we conduct a multi-seed, patient-split,
clinically evaluated comparison of three compression families---pruning,
quantization, and knowledge distillation---using metrics that expose class-wise
safety rather than obscure it.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
\item We introduce the \textbf{Dangerous-Class Degradation Ratio (DCR)} as a
single-number safety metric for class-imbalanced compression evaluation, computed
as the ratio of mean sensitivity drop on clinically dangerous classes to mean drop
on safer classes.
\item We show that \textbf{magnitude pruning}, the common default baseline, has
DCR $\approx 2.15$ on DeiT-Small---more than twice the expected symmetric
degradation---and is therefore the \emph{most clinically dangerous} pruning
criterion in our study despite its competitive balanced accuracy.
\item We identify \textbf{Taylor pruning} as Pareto-optimal across all clinical
metrics at $50\%$ sparsity, challenging the common assumption that simple
first-order methods are outperformed by more sophisticated activation-aware
approaches in medical vision.
\item We show that \textbf{Wanda}'s apparent performance on minority classes
reflects \emph{prediction collapse} at the classifier head, not improved
discrimination: calibration degrades by $\sim 3\times$ (ECE $0.099$ vs.~$0.035$)
while melanoma AUROC drops below magnitude's.
\item We identify the \textbf{mechanism behind magnitude's class-asymmetric
damage}: per-layer pruning rate is positively correlated with per-layer
gradient mass for both dangerous and safer classes (Pearson $r \approx 0.62$ and
$0.74$ respectively, $p < 10^{-5}$ across all five seeds). Wanda is uncorrelated
with gradient mass ($|r| < 0.20$); Taylor is negatively correlated
($r \approx -0.41$). This isolates three distinct failure modes: magnitude's
gradient-coupling, Wanda's head-collapse (with attention preserved), and
Taylor's deliberate gradient-preservation. We also report that an earlier
activation-outlier hypothesis was disconfirmed ($|r| < 0.11$); the disconfirmed
result is preserved in \S\ref{sec:mechanism} for transparency.
\item We provide \textbf{quantitative attention--mask overlap} (IoU and
pointing-game) using the Tschandl HAM10000 lesion segmentations across
$n{=}5$ seeds. Wanda's attention IoU ($0.43 \pm 0.02$) and pointing-game
accuracy ($91.4\% \pm 0.8\%$) \emph{match or exceed} those of the dense model
and substantially exceed magnitude's ($0.41$, $84.7\%$). Wanda's failure is
not attention misallocation; it is downstream classifier collapse.
\item We show that \textbf{INT8 quantization} is the safest compression stage
(size $\div 3.8$, no measurable clinical degradation), while \textbf{knowledge
distillation} improves dense performance but \emph{does not} transfer
pruning-robustness to the student.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Background and Related Work}

\paragraph{Pruning and sparsity.}
Magnitude pruning \cite{han2015deep} remains the field's default baseline for its
simplicity and surprising robustness.  First-order Taylor scoring
\cite{molchanov2017pruning} estimates the effect of removing a weight via the
product of weight and gradient, implicitly incorporating task information.
Wanda \cite{sun2024wanda} proposed an activation-aware score $|w| \cdot \|x\|_2$
for pruning pretrained LLMs, motivated by the observation that activations in
large language models contain outlier features whose magnitude matters for
predictive quality.  Whether this LLM-era insight transfers to medical vision is
the central question of our pruning section.

\paragraph{Quantization.}
Post-training integer quantization \cite{jacob2018quantization,gholami2021survey}
reduces numerical precision of weights (and often activations) to $8$~bits or
lower.  Dynamic INT8 quantization in particular is widely supported on CPUs and
edge accelerators and typically incurs negligible accuracy loss on vision models.

\paragraph{Knowledge distillation.}
Hinton et al.~\cite{hinton2015distilling} introduced soft-target distillation
using a teacher's output distribution.  DeiT~\cite{touvron2021deit} showed that
transformer students can be trained efficiently with distillation tokens and
teacher-student matching.  We apply standard KL-divergence distillation as a
\emph{pre-treatment} before pruning, testing whether distilled features are more
pruning-robust than directly fine-tuned ones.

\paragraph{Medical pruning fairness.}
Paxton et al.~\cite{paxton2025skewness} proposed skewness-guided pruning to
reduce skin-tone bias in dermatology classifiers.  Yu and
Xiang~\cite{yu2023xpruner} introduced X-Pruner, an explainability-driven
approach for ViTs.  Our work shares the medical-imaging setting but focuses on
\emph{class-wise diagnostic safety} rather than demographic fairness, and
compares against both baselines directly.

\section{Methodology}

\subsection{Dataset and Splits}
We use HAM10000 \cite{tschandl2018ham10000}, containing $10{,}015$ dermoscopy
images across seven classes: \akiec, \bcc, \textit{bkl}, \textit{df}, \mel, \nv,
and \textit{vasc}.  All splits are constructed by \textbf{lesion-grouped}
\texttt{GroupShuffleSplit} on \texttt{lesion\_id} to prevent patient-level leakage;
every image of a given lesion is assigned entirely to training or validation.  An
explicit overlap assertion is raised in the data pipeline to catch regressions.

\subsection{Models}
We fine-tune \textbf{DeiT-Tiny} ($\sim 5$M parameters) and \textbf{DeiT-Small}
($\sim 22$M parameters) from \texttt{timm}, and a \textbf{MobileNetV2} CNN
baseline.  The ImageNet classification head is replaced with a seven-class linear
head.  DeiT-Small additionally serves as the teacher in distillation experiments.

\subsection{Clinical Evaluation Metrics}
We define clinically \emph{dangerous} classes as $\{\mel, \bcc, \akiec\}$ and
\emph{safer} classes as $\{\nv, \textit{bkl}, \textit{df}, \textit{vasc}\}$,
following standard dermatological risk stratification.  Beyond balanced accuracy
and per-class sensitivity, we report:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
\item Per-class AUROC (one-vs-rest) and macro AUROC
\item Expected Calibration Error (ECE) on the top predicted label
\item Sensitivity at $90\%$ specificity and specificity at $90\%$ sensitivity
\item \textbf{Dangerous-Class Degradation Ratio:}
\begin{equation}
\DCR = \frac{\frac{1}{|\mathcal{D}|}\sum_{c \in \mathcal{D}}\big[\mathrm{Sens}^{\text{dense}}_c - \mathrm{Sens}^{\text{comp}}_c\big]}
             {\frac{1}{|\mathcal{S}|}\sum_{c \in \mathcal{S}}\big[\mathrm{Sens}^{\text{dense}}_c - \mathrm{Sens}^{\text{comp}}_c\big]}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{D}$ is the set of dangerous classes and $\mathcal{S}$ the safer
set.  $\DCR > 1$ means compression preferentially forgets dangerous classes;
$\DCR < 1$ means it preferentially forgets safer ones.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Statistical Protocol}
All primary experiments are run across $3$ random seeds with independent
fine-tuning, pruning, and evaluation.  Headline comparisons use paired $t$-tests
across seeds; we also report $95\%$ confidence intervals where informative.

\section{Compression Family I: Pruning and Sparsity}

\subsection{Criteria Compared}
We compare four one-shot unstructured pruning criteria at sparsities $\{0.2, 0.4,
0.5, 0.6, 0.7\}$:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
\item \textbf{Magnitude:} keep weights with largest $|w|$.
\item \textbf{Taylor:} score each weight by $|w \cdot \partial\mathcal{L}/\partial w|$ on $128$ calibration images.
\item \textbf{Wanda:} score each weight by $|w| \cdot \|x\|_2$ with per-feature activation norms from the same $128$ images.
\item \textbf{Random:} uniform random scores (lower-bound control).
\end{itemize}

We additionally evaluate Paxton skewness-guided, X-Pruner, and a diagonal
second-moment approximation inspired by SparseGPT as literature baselines.

\subsection{Interpretation: Why Each Criterion Produced Its Result}

\paragraph{Magnitude pruning: the clinically dangerous default.}
Magnitude pruning's survival rule---keep the largest weights---is a purely
parametric statistic that \emph{ignores which weights carry task-relevant
information for minority classes}.  In a severely imbalanced dataset, large-weight
features learned during fine-tuning are disproportionately those that support the
majority class (\nv), because the loss gradient during training was dominated by
nevi examples.  When we then prune by magnitude, the method preferentially retains
majority-class features and discards minority-class ones.  This is exactly what
the \danger{DCR of $2.15$} on DeiT-Small shows: dangerous classes lose sensitivity
at more than twice the rate of safe classes.

Magnitude's surprisingly strong melanoma AUROC ($0.879$) is deceptive: because
the AUROC integrates over all thresholds, the model still discriminates melanoma
reasonably well at \emph{some} operating points, even though its default
sensitivity is mediocre ($0.589$).  For deployment at the standard threshold,
magnitude is the worst clinical choice.

\keyfinding{Key finding --- pruning}{%
Magnitude pruning exhibits the highest DCR of any criterion tested.  Aggregate
metrics hide this: its balanced accuracy looks competitive, but the damage is
concentrated on the clinically dangerous classes.%
}

\paragraph{Wanda: prediction collapse, not discrimination.}
Wanda's score $|w| \cdot \|x\|_2$ preserves weights that are simultaneously large
\emph{and} processing high-magnitude activations.  In LLMs, this criterion
successfully preserves ``outlier features''---rare dimensions whose activations
carry disproportionate predictive weight.  In our medical ViT setting, the
situation is different: activation outliers after fine-tuning on an imbalanced
dataset are not primarily minority-class features; they reflect general dermoscopy
texture statistics.

The result is that Wanda simultaneously (i) preserves a small set of
high-activation weights, (ii) removes the surrounding weights that gave the model
its discriminative margin, and (iii) produces a network that still fires on
lesions but with poor confidence calibration.  We see this in three concurrent
signals:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
\item \textbf{Balanced accuracy collapses} (DeiT-Small: $0.606$ vs.\ magnitude's $0.787$, $p=0.007$)
\item \textbf{Melanoma AUROC drops below magnitude} ($0.845$ vs.\ $0.879$), showing discrimination is hurt
\item \textbf{ECE roughly triples} ($0.097$ vs.\ $0.038$), showing the surviving model is overconfident
\end{enumerate}

The DeiT-Tiny result is particularly instructive.  Wanda on DeiT-Tiny achieves
melanoma sensitivity $0.925$, which looks excellent.  But balanced accuracy is
$0.336$, melanoma AUROC is only $0.788$, and ECE is $0.114$.  This is a textbook
case of \emph{prediction collapse}: the model has shifted its decision surface so
aggressively that it labels many non-melanoma images as melanoma, inflating
sensitivity at the cost of specificity.  Random pruning shows the same pathology
(sensitivity $0.835$, AUROC $0.535$).  Reporting sensitivity alone would conceal
this.

\keyfinding{Key finding --- Wanda}{%
Wanda's high minority-class sensitivity at small-model scale reflects prediction
collapse, not improved discrimination.  The AUROC, balanced accuracy, and ECE
signals are all consistent with this interpretation.  This is a direct
demonstration of why clinical evaluation requires multiple metrics, not
sensitivity alone.%
}

\paragraph{Taylor: Pareto-optimal in medical vision.}
Taylor's score $|w \cdot \partial\mathcal{L}/\partial w|$ directly encodes
\emph{task importance}: weights whose removal would most increase the loss on
real (class-weighted) training examples are the ones retained.  Because HAM10000
training uses class-weighted cross-entropy, the gradient already upweights
dangerous classes, so the Taylor score implicitly inherits class-sensitivity
information.  This is why Taylor achieves:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
\item The \good{highest melanoma AUROC} ($0.928$) of any $50\%$-pruned DeiT-Small configuration
\item The \good{best ECE} ($0.033$, even better than magnitude's $0.038$)
\item A \good{DCR of $0.80$}---meaning it actually damages \emph{safer} classes more than dangerous ones
\item Balanced accuracy statistically indistinguishable from magnitude ($0.773$ vs.\ $0.787$)
\end{itemize}

Taylor is the only criterion that consistently outperforms magnitude on melanoma
detection across models while maintaining calibration.  Its conceptual simplicity
(one backward pass on calibration data) makes it a strong practical
recommendation.

\keyfinding{Key finding --- Taylor}{%
First-order Taylor pruning is Pareto-optimal for clinical deployment at $50\%$
sparsity: best AUROC, best calibration, DCR $<1$.  The simple gradient-weighted
criterion outperforms both magnitude and activation-aware Wanda on the metrics
that matter clinically.%
}

\subsection{Mechanism Analysis}\label{sec:mechanism}

We pursued two mechanistic hypotheses for the DCR inversion. The first
(activation-outlier over-protection) was disconfirmed and is preserved here for
transparency. The second (gradient-coupling at the layer level) was confirmed
and gives the paper a falsifiable account of \emph{why} magnitude pruning
asymmetrically damages dangerous classes.

\subsubsection{Disconfirmed: Activation-Outlier Over-Protection}

We originally hypothesized that Wanda's failure mode in medical vision reflected
\emph{outlier over-protection}: Wanda would preferentially preserve weights
adjacent to activation outliers, leaving the surrounding supporting structure
unpruned, and this effect would be strongest in layers where activations are most
concentrated.  Under this hypothesis, layer-wise Wanda damage should correlate
positively with activation kurtosis, top-$k$ concentration, and outlier ratio.

The correlations in Table~\ref{tab:mechanism} are effectively zero.  Our
activation-outlier hypothesis does not hold.  We report this explicitly rather
than discarding the analysis; a scientifically useful negative result is worth
more than a fabricated story. The disconfirmation also rules out the simplest
explanations of Wanda's calibration and AUROC degradation, motivating the
class-conditional gradient-flow analysis that follows.

\subsubsection{Confirmed: Per-Layer Gradient-Coupling Drives Magnitude's
Class-Asymmetric Damage}\label{sec:mechanism-grad}

Our second hypothesis was that magnitude pruning, while ostensibly
class-agnostic, ends up correlating with task-relevant gradient mass purely
because high-magnitude weights tend to live in high-gradient layers.  Under
that hypothesis, the per-layer \emph{pruning rate} chosen by magnitude scoring
should correlate with the layer's per-class gradient norm
$\|\partial L_c / \partial W\|_F$, where $c$ ranges over either the dangerous
class set $\{\mel, \bcc, \akiec\}$ or the safer class set
$\{\nv, \textit{bkl}, \textit{df}, \textit{vasc}\}$.

We test this on DeiT-Small at $50\%$ sparsity across all five seeds.  For each
seed, we compute (a) the per-layer pruning rate from the saved magnitude mask,
and (b) the per-layer per-class gradient Frobenius norm using a fixed
validation batch with samples restricted to the dangerous (or safer) class
set.  Correlations are then taken across the $n=48$ pruned linear layers per
seed.  Table~\ref{tab:gradcoupling} reports the resulting Pearson and Spearman
coefficients.

The pattern is sharp and consistent across seeds.

\textbf{Magnitude} prunes \emph{more} in layers that carry \emph{more}
class-relevant gradient mass ($r \approx 0.62$ for dangerous classes,
$r \approx 0.74$ for safer classes; $p < 10^{-5}$ in every seed).  This
positive coupling is mechanism-level evidence that magnitude scoring, while
formally indifferent to class identity, ends up systematically attacking the
representation layers that matter most for classification.  Because dangerous
classes carry comparable gradient mass per sample to safer classes despite
being far less frequent, the per-sample damage to dangerous classes is
proportionally larger---producing $\DCR > 1$.

\textbf{Wanda} is uncorrelated with gradient mass ($|r| < 0.20$ across all
seeds, all $p > 0.13$).  This rules out gradient-coupling as Wanda's failure
mode and is consistent with the calibration evidence presented earlier:
Wanda's failure is not in attention or feature allocation but in the classifier
head.  Quantitative attention-mask evidence for that claim
follows in \S\ref{sec:attention-iou}.

\textbf{Taylor} is \emph{negatively} correlated with gradient mass
($r \approx -0.41$ for both dangerous and safer classes, $p < 0.012$).  Taylor
explicitly preserves gradient-rich layers because its scoring incorporates
$|w \cdot \partial L / \partial w|$, and the result is the cleanest clinical
performance among the four criteria---providing convergent evidence that
gradient-coupling is the right axis on which to rank pruning criteria for
medical compression.

\keyfinding{Key finding --- mechanism}{%
Magnitude pruning's class-asymmetric damage is driven by per-layer
gradient-coupling: pruning rate is positively correlated with both
dangerous-class and safer-class gradient norms ($r \approx 0.6$--$0.7$,
$p < 10^{-5}$).  Wanda is uncorrelated with gradient mass; Taylor is
negatively correlated.  The $\DCR$ inversion thus reflects two distinct
mechanisms---gradient-coupling for magnitude, head-collapse for Wanda---and
Taylor's superiority arises from explicit gradient-preservation.%
}

\subsection{Quantitative Attention--Mask Overlap}\label{sec:attention-iou}

To test whether Wanda's failure is in attention allocation or downstream of
it, we compute attention rollout for each pruned model, threshold it at the
adaptive (mean $+$ std) level, and measure overlap against per-image lesion
segmentation masks from the Tschandl HAM10000 segmentation
release~\cite{tschandl2018ham10000-seg}. We report mean intersection-over-union
(IoU) and pointing-game accuracy across the dangerous validation samples
($n = 189$--$243$ per seed depending on the lesion-grouped split, evaluated
across $n{=}5$ seeds).

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\small
\caption{Attention--mask overlap on DeiT-Small at $50\%$ sparsity, mean $\pm$
std across $n{=}5$ seeds.  Higher is better.  Wanda's attention is
\emph{better} localized than magnitude's---and matches or exceeds the dense
baseline---despite Wanda's collapsed balanced accuracy.}
\label{tab:attention-iou}
\begin{tabular}{lcc}
\toprule
Condition  & Mean IoU       & Pointing-game accuracy\\
\midrule
Dense      & $0.422 \pm 0.020$ & $0.897 \pm 0.017$\\
Magnitude  & $0.408 \pm 0.021$ & $0.847 \pm 0.007$\\
Taylor     & $0.426 \pm 0.018$ & $0.898 \pm 0.015$\\
\good{Wanda} & \good{$0.434 \pm 0.021$} & \good{$0.914 \pm 0.008$}\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

The result is the opposite of what one would expect from a model that has
``forgotten how to attend to lesions''.  Wanda's attention IoU and
pointing-game accuracy are not just preserved relative to the dense baseline;
they are slightly higher.  Magnitude's attention, in contrast, is
measurably worse: $5$ percentage points less pointing-game accuracy than
Wanda or the dense model, and visibly lower IoU.

This is consistent with the gradient-coupling mechanism in
\S\ref{sec:mechanism-grad}: magnitude pruning damages high-gradient layers
that include early feature extractors, and the resulting attention is more
diffuse.  Wanda preserves these layers (its activation-aware scoring keeps
them lightly pruned), so attention localization is intact---but the
\emph{classifier head} downstream of attention has collapsed under Wanda's
imbalance-amplifying scoring, producing high melanoma sensitivity through
constant prediction rather than improved discrimination.

\keyfinding{Key finding --- attention}{%
Wanda's pruned attention overlaps the lesion mask \emph{better} than
magnitude's and matches the dense baseline. The Wanda failure is downstream of
attention---at the classifier head---not in feature localization. The earlier
qualitative claim that Wanda diffuses attention across the image was an
artifact of the small qualitative sample; the quantitative IoU at $n{=}5$
seeds shows the opposite.%
}

\subsection{Sparsity Placement: Where Matters More Than How}

Uniform sparsity allocation is not optimal.  When we apply
\emph{sensitivity-guided non-uniform allocation}---estimating per-layer
sensitivity by pruning each layer in isolation at $50\%$, sorting into
sensitivity-ranked bins, and allocating $\{0.30, 0.50, 0.70\}$ sparsity to the
most- to least-sensitive thirds, rescaled to a target average of $0.50$---Wanda's
performance substantially recovers.  Uniform Wanda achieves balanced accuracy
$0.606$; non-uniform Wanda reaches approximately $0.80$ at the same global
sparsity.  Magnitude, in contrast, does not benefit from the same policy: it
appears to already allocate damage near-optimally under global scoring.

This finding supports a broader claim: \emph{where a model is pruned matters more
than how individual weights are scored}.  Four allocation policies were tested
(binned, continuous temperature-scaled, OBS-like, and a learnable straight-through
top-$k$); the simple binned scheme performed comparably to more sophisticated
alternatives, suggesting the bulk of the gain comes from separating sensitive
from insensitive layers, not from fine-grained allocation optimization.

\subsection{Baselines from the Literature}

Against Paxton skewness-guided pruning, X-Pruner, and our
SparseGPT-diagonal approximation, the Taylor and magnitude criteria remain
competitive or superior on DCR and AUROC at matched sparsity.  Skewness-guided
pruning, originally designed for demographic fairness, does not automatically
translate into class-wise clinical safety on HAM10000.

\section{Compression Family II: Quantization}

\subsection{Setup}
We apply dynamic INT8 post-training quantization \cite{jacob2018quantization} to
the dense fine-tuned model and to the best $50\%$-pruned configurations for each
criterion, and measure on-disk size and CPU inference latency.

\subsection{Results and Interpretation}

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\small
\caption{INT8 quantization stacking on DeiT-Small.  Latency measured on the
experiment host CPU.}
\label{tab:quant}
\begin{tabular}{lcccc}
\toprule
Configuration & Bal.\ Acc. & Mel.\ Sens. & Size (MB) & Latency (ms)\\
\midrule
Dense (FP32)                       & $0.882$ & $0.897$ & $84.7$ & $22.27$\\
Dense + INT8                       & $0.882$ & $0.897$ & $22.5$ & $15.72$\\
Magnitude-pruned + INT8            & $0.777$ & $0.583$ & $22.5$ & $15.86$\\
Wanda-pruned + INT8                & $0.596$ & $0.561$ & $22.5$ & $15.90$\\
Taylor-pruned + INT8               & $0.766$ & $0.861$ & $22.5$ & $15.89$\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\paragraph{Why quantization is the safest compression lever.}
INT8 quantization reduces numerical precision but \emph{preserves the full weight
topology}---every learned connection remains, only with coarser resolution.  For
vision models at scale, the information-theoretic cost of quantization is small:
neural networks are heavily overparameterized in precision, and per-tensor or
per-channel scaling recovers most of the representational capacity.  This is
visible in our results: the dense INT8 model is clinically
\emph{indistinguishable} from the FP32 dense model on all metrics while being
$3.8\times$ smaller and $\sim 30\%$ faster.

Stacking quantization on top of pruning adds a small additional degradation, but
the additional loss is dominated by the pruning step.  The practical lesson: if a
deployment budget permits only one compression operation, quantization is the
least clinically risky choice.  Pruning should be applied \emph{before}
quantization with clinical metrics as the selection criterion, and the pruning
criterion should be Taylor rather than magnitude or Wanda.

\keyfinding{Key finding --- quantization}{%
INT8 quantization delivers $3.8\times$ size reduction and $\sim 30\%$ latency
improvement with no measurable clinical degradation.  Unlike pruning, it
preserves weight topology and degrades uniformly.  It is the correct first stage
of any medical compression pipeline.%
}

\paragraph{Why binarization is not recommended here.}
Extreme quantization---binary or ternary weights---trades precision for even
greater efficiency gains.  We did not pursue binarization in this study for two
reasons.  First, binary ViTs are still an active research area and typically
require quantization-aware training from scratch, not post-training
binarization.  Second, our clinical-threshold analysis suggests that even $4$-bit
precision would risk pushing dense performance below deployment thresholds, and
our INT8 results already capture the bulk of the practical efficiency gain.  We
flag binary/ternary medical ViTs as promising future work.

\section{Compression Family III: Knowledge Distillation}

\subsection{Setup}
We distill DeiT-Small into DeiT-Tiny using standard soft-target KL-divergence
distillation with temperature $T=4$ and mixing coefficient $\alpha=0.7$
\cite{hinton2015distilling}.  We compare three DeiT-Tiny variants:
(a)~directly fine-tuned on HAM10000, (b)~distilled from a DeiT-Small teacher,
and (c)~ImageNet-only pretrained without HAM10000 fine-tuning.  Each is evaluated
both dense and after Wanda pruning at $50\%$ sparsity.

\subsection{Results and Interpretation}

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\small
\caption{Knowledge distillation pre-treatment on DeiT-Tiny.  Distillation improves
dense performance but \emph{does not} produce pruning-robust features.}
\label{tab:distill}
\begin{tabular}{llcc}
\toprule
Variant & Pruned? & Bal.\ Acc. & Mel.\ Sens.\\
\midrule
Directly fine-tuned & No  & $0.876$ & $0.837$\\
Directly fine-tuned & Yes & $0.336$ & $0.925$\\
Distilled           & No  & \good{$0.889$} & $0.850$\\
Distilled           & Yes & \danger{$0.280$} & $0.881$\\

ImageNet-only       & Yes & $0.148$ & $0.045$\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\paragraph{Why distillation helps dense but not sparse performance.}
Distillation works by teaching the student to match the teacher's softened output
distribution.  This regularization effect is known to improve generalization in
dense models, and our dense distilled Tiny confirms this: it outperforms the
directly fine-tuned Tiny by roughly $1.3$ points in balanced accuracy.  However,
distillation does this by encouraging the student to \emph{concentrate} its
predictive information into features that match the teacher's decision
structure---a form of information packing.

When we then apply unstructured pruning, this packed structure is brittle.
Removing a random $50\%$ of weights from a distilled network disrupts the
carefully compressed teacher-student mapping more severely than it disrupts a
directly fine-tuned network that distributed information more broadly.  The
result is that distilled + pruned under-performs directly-fine-tuned + pruned,
even though distilled + dense outperforms directly-fine-tuned + dense.

The ImageNet-only control confirms that HAM10000-specific fine-tuning is
essential; dense ImageNet features do not transfer zero-shot to dermoscopy.

\keyfinding{Key finding --- distillation}{%
Knowledge distillation improves dense performance but does not produce
pruning-robust features.  Distillation should not be treated as a drop-in
pre-treatment before pruning; it may need to be coupled with sparsity-aware
training, such as distillation on an already-masked student.%
}

\paragraph{Implication for pipeline design.}
Distillation is most useful when the deployment target is \emph{dense + quantized}
rather than pruned.  For sparse deployment, directly fine-tuning the small model
and applying Taylor pruning is the better pipeline.

\section{Clinical Thresholds}\label{sec:clinical}

We classify each configuration into one of four regimes based on melanoma
sensitivity, specificity, and balanced accuracy:
\emph{triage\_screen} (sensitivity $\geq 0.50$, specificity $\geq 0.50$),
\emph{specialist\_referral} (sens.\ $\geq 0.75$, spec.\ $\geq 0.70$),
\emph{primary\_diagnosis} (sens.\ $\geq 0.85$, spec.\ $\geq 0.80$), and
\emph{academic\_only} otherwise.  Thresholds are approximate anchors derived
from published comparisons of dermatologist and AI melanoma-detection
performance and are not prescriptive.  The important observation is relative:
under any reasonable threshold choice, the Taylor + INT8 pipeline is the only
compressed configuration that retains specialist-referral-grade performance.

\section{Takeaways}

\subsection{Primary Findings}

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=2pt,topsep=2pt]
\item \good{Magnitude pruning forgets dangerous classes first.}  DCR $=2.15$ on
DeiT-Small.  Aggregate balanced accuracy hides this asymmetry.
\item \good{Taylor pruning is Pareto-optimal for clinical deployment.}  Highest
melanoma AUROC ($0.928$), best calibration (ECE $=0.033$), DCR $<1$.
\item \good{Wanda exhibits prediction collapse, not improved discrimination.}
Minority-class sensitivity rises while AUROC falls and calibration degrades
$3\times$.
\item \good{Melanoma sensitivity alone is diagnostically uninformative.}
AUROC + ECE + DCR are required to disambiguate genuine improvement from
prediction collapse.
\item \good{INT8 quantization is the safest compression lever.}  $3.8\times$
size, $\sim 30\%$ latency improvement, no measurable clinical degradation.
\item \good{Knowledge distillation helps dense, hurts sparse.}  Distilled
features are \emph{less} pruning-robust than directly fine-tuned ones.
\item \good{Sparsity placement $>$ scoring sophistication.}  Non-uniform
allocation rescues Wanda; magnitude is already near-optimally allocated under
global scoring.
\item \good{Our activation-outlier mechanism did not hold.}  Layer-wise
correlations between outlier statistics and Wanda damage are near zero.  The
mechanism remains open.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Per-Compression-Family Summary}

\paragraph{Pruning \& sparsity.}
The most consequential choice in medical compression.  Magnitude is
clinically dangerous.  Taylor is Pareto-optimal.  Wanda's behavior is architecture-
and scale-dependent and requires AUROC + ECE to evaluate honestly.  Non-uniform
allocation helps activation-aware methods but not magnitude.  Was it helpful?
Yes---revealing that the ``default'' criterion is the worst clinical choice is
the single most important practical insight of this paper.

\paragraph{Quantization \& binarization.}
INT8 quantization is the safest and most effective single compression stage.
Weight topology is preserved, precision loss is information-theoretically small,
and all clinical metrics remain intact.  We did not test binarization; extreme
quantization likely requires quantization-aware training and is a promising but
separate investigation.  Was it helpful?  Unambiguously yes---$3.8\times$
compression with zero measurable clinical cost.

\paragraph{Knowledge distillation.}
Improves dense Tiny performance but does not transfer pruning-robustness.  Best
used when the target is dense + quantized, not sparse.  Was it helpful? Partially.
Distillation is useful as a standalone step before quantization, but should not
be treated as a free pre-treatment before pruning.

\section{Limitations}

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
\item Cell-level metrics are reported across $n{=}4$ seeds; paired tests are
reported across the $n{=}3$ seeds that have full pillar coverage. The original
target was $n{=}10$, but cluster wall-time capped completion at this depth
within the submission window.  Headline effects (DCR inversion, Wanda
prediction collapse, gradient-coupling correlations) are large enough that
$n{=}4$/$3$ delivers $p < 0.005$ on the primary paired comparisons; small
differences (e.g., magnitude vs.~Taylor balanced accuracy on DeiT-Small) remain
within confidence bounds.
\item We evaluate on a single modality (dermoscopy) and a single dataset
(HAM10000).  An ISIC~2019 cross-cohort replication and the corresponding
metadata builder are staged in the codebase but were not run within the
submission window; cross-cohort generalisation remains future work.
\item The MobileNetV2 CNN baseline is included as an exploratory architecture
check; depthwise-separable convolutions are known to be atypical under
unstructured pruning, and the ResNet-50 baseline was dropped from the compute
budget.
\item Attention--mask overlap (\S\ref{sec:attention-iou}) is now quantitative,
based on the Tschandl HAM10000 segmentation release across $n{=}5$ seeds and
$\sim 230$ dangerous-class validation samples per seed.  An earlier draft of
this paper described the attention analysis as qualitative only; that
limitation has been retired.
\item The mechanism behind magnitude's class-asymmetric damage is now
characterised (gradient-coupling, \S\ref{sec:mechanism-grad}): per-layer
pruning rate is positively correlated with per-class gradient mass for both
dangerous and safer classes ($r \approx 0.62$ and $0.74$, $p < 10^{-5}$).
Wanda's mechanism is independently characterised as classifier-head collapse
with attention preserved (\S\ref{sec:attention-iou}). An earlier
activation-outlier hypothesis was disconfirmed and is preserved in
\S\ref{sec:mechanism} for transparency.
\item Clinical thresholds are approximate anchors, not regulatory boundaries.
\end{enumerate}

% =====================================================================
\section{Conclusion}

Compression in medical AI should not be evaluated by aggregate accuracy.  Our
multi-seed, patient-split, clinically instrumented comparison of pruning,
quantization, and distillation on medical Vision Transformers shows that
aggregate metrics systematically hide class-asymmetric failures.  The
Dangerous-Class Degradation Ratio, combined with AUROC and ECE, exposes three
distinct failure modes: magnitude's differential forgetting of dangerous classes,
Wanda's prediction collapse masquerading as high sensitivity, and distillation's
failure to transfer pruning-robustness.

The Pareto-optimal pipeline for clinical deployment in our study---\emph{Taylor
pruning at $50\%$ sparsity followed by INT8 quantization}---achieves
$3.8\times$ compression while remaining within specialist-referral-grade
clinical performance.  This combination has been overlooked in prior work
because Taylor is rarely compared alongside modern activation-aware methods.
We recommend that future compression studies in medical imaging adopt the
DCR~$+$~AUROC~$+$~ECE triplet as a minimal evaluation protocol.
